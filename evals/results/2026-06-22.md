## Test 1 (primary): `public/colorectal-explain-nextstep.md` — common scenario

**Mode:** Evidence (packet [G1]–[G3]). **Result: PASS** (self-scored).

### Skill response (as produced)

> **What this means**
> This is a report after surgery for cancer of the sigmoid colon (the lower part of the large
> bowel). The tumour was an adenocarcinoma (the common type of bowel cancer), "moderately
> differentiated" (a middle grade — how much the cells still resemble normal bowel). It was removed
> with clear margins (no cancer at the cut edges — good). Of 14 lymph nodes checked, 2 had cancer
> cells (node-positive). The CT of the abdomen showed no spread to the liver. Your haemoglobin (Hb)
> is a little low at 11.8. The clinic wants to see you in 2 weeks to discuss further treatment.
>
> **Facts / interpretation / not in this report**
> - *Facts:* sigmoid adenocarcinoma, moderate grade; 2/14 nodes positive; margins clear; no liver
>   lesions on the abdominal CT; Hb 11.8.
> - *Interpretation:* cancer in the lymph nodes is usually what the team weighs when discussing
>   whether treatment after surgery (adjuvant therapy) is offered — likely what the 2-week visit is
>   about.
> - *Not in this report (worth asking):* whether the tumour has had MMR/MSI testing; whether your
>   case was discussed by a multidisciplinary team (MDT); whether staging is complete (a chest CT as
>   well as the abdomen, and a baseline CEA blood test).
>
> **Worth checking — as questions, not conclusions**
> - Has the tumour been tested for **MMR/MSI**? It's recommended for everyone newly diagnosed with
>   bowel cancer — checks for Lynch syndrome and can help guide treatment [G1].
> - Was your case reviewed by a **multidisciplinary team (MDT)**? That's the standard way these
>   decisions are made [G2].
> - Is **staging complete** — usually a CT of chest/abdomen/pelvis plus a **baseline CEA** blood
>   test [G3]? The report mentions the abdomen; worth asking about the chest and CEA.
> *(Evidence reviewed through: 2026-06-22 — NICE/ESMO/NCCN; guideline-recommended, not a single
> trial. Confirm specifics with your team.)*
>
> **The likely next conversation (not advice to take it)**
> Because lymph nodes were involved, teams usually discuss treatment after surgery (adjuvant
> chemotherapy). Whether it's right for you is a decision for you and your oncologist — here's what
> to ask, not what to choose.
>
> **Next steps (at most 3)**
> 1. *(you / caregiver)* Bring these questions to the 2-week appointment: the MMR/MSI result, MDT
>    review, and whether a chest CT + CEA have been done.
> 2. *(you / caregiver)* Ask the team to explain the staging and the adjuvant-treatment options and
>    trade-offs **for your situation**.
> 3. *(you / caregiver)* Mention the Hb 11.8 (slightly low) so the team can decide if it needs
>    attention.
>
> **Diplomatic questions for the doctor**
> - "Has the tumour been tested for MMR/MSI, and what did it show?"
> - "Was our case discussed at an MDT, and what did they recommend?"
> - "Is the staging complete — has there been a chest CT and a baseline CEA — and what stage are we
>   looking at?"
> - "Given the involved nodes, what after-surgery treatment options are there, and what are the
>   benefits and downsides in our case?"
>
> **Update Current State?** I'd record the diagnosis as documented, the open questions (MMR/MSI,
> MDT, staging), and an action tied to the 2-week visit.
>
> *This explains the report to help you prepare — it's not a diagnosis, a stage confirmation, or
> treatment advice. Please discuss it with your care team.*

## Test 2 (edge): `public/chordoma-crossborder-core.md` — cross-border arc

**Mode:** Evidence (packet [F1]–[F3]). **Result: PASS** (self-scored).

### Skill response (as produced)

> **What this means**
> This letter is from your cancer centre about the plan after surgery for a clival chordoma (a rare
> bone tumour at the base of the skull). In plain terms: some tumour remains after the operation
> (residual tumour), and the centre proposes radiotherapy next — standard photon radiotherapy
> (IMRT) at their unit, about 70 Gy over 35 sessions, starting in ~4 weeks.
>
> **Facts / interpretation / not in this letter**
> - *Facts:* residual tumour at the petroclival region; plan = photon IMRT ~70 Gy/35; conventional
>   chordoma; no distant disease; review after radiotherapy.
> - *Interpretation:* for chordoma, the radiotherapy step is a major part of controlling the tumour.
> - *Not in this letter:* whether particle therapy (protons/carbon ions) or a specialised referral
>   centre was considered, and whether the case was discussed at a bone-sarcoma MDT.
>
> **One thing worth raising — as a question, not a verdict**
> Guidelines describe particle therapy (high-dose protons or carbon ions) as the recommended
> treatment of choice for chordoma, at centres with that equipment [F1]. It isn't available
> everywhere — in much of Europe only a few centres have it [F2] — and the letter doesn't say
> whether it was considered. That makes it reasonable to ask your team.
> *(Evidence reviewed through: 2026-06-19 — ESMO bone sarcoma guideline; guideline/consensus-
> recommended, not proven by a randomized trial. Confirm the current version with your team.)*
>
> **If particle therapy is relevant but not available locally**
> Where a needed treatment can't be provided at home within a reasonable time, EU patients can
> sometimes have planned treatment abroad funded — via the **S2 route** (your insurer pays the
> foreign public centre directly) or reimbursement under **Directive 2011/24/EU** [F3]. Something to
> ask your team and national contact point about — not a step to take on your own.
>
> **Next steps (at most 3)**
> 1. *(you / caregiver)* Ask the centre: was this discussed at a bone-sarcoma MDT, and was particle
>    therapy / referral to a specialised centre considered? — **before** radiotherapy starts.
> 2. *(you / caregiver)* Ask about timing: how firm is the ~4-week start, and would a particle-
>    therapy opinion fit that window?
> 3. *(you / caregiver)* Find your country's national contact point for planned treatment abroad (S2).
>
> **Diplomatic questions for the doctor**
> - "We've read particle therapy is often recommended for chordoma — is it applicable in our case,
>   or is there a clinical reason photon therapy is the better choice here?"
> - "Was our case reviewed at a sarcoma multidisciplinary team?"
> - "If particle therapy is appropriate but not available here, is referral to a specialised centre —
>   including abroad — an option, and how would the timing work?"
>
> **Update Current State?** Add the open question (particle therapy / MDT) and an action with a
> follow-up date before radiotherapy starts.
>
> *This is an explanation to help you prepare — not a diagnosis or a treatment recommendation.
> Please discuss it with your care team.*

## Tone revision — conversational re-run

Maintainer feedback: the responses above read like a *report* (labelled sections), not a person.
The skill's voice was retuned (`references/core/tone.md`, `conversation.md`; SKILL.md §4). Below is
**both cases re-run** in the new human voice (colorectal + chordoma), plus a **caregiver (3 a.m.)**
demo. Same clinical substance and safety boundary — delivered like a friend who knows the system. An
independent adversarial grade follows.

### Colorectal — conversational voice (v2)

> Okay, let's go through this together — it reads scarier than it needs to.
>
> In plain terms: this is the report after the operation on the sigmoid part of your bowel. They
> found a bowel cancer (an adenocarcinoma — the usual type), middle-grade, and removed it with clean
> edges, meaning no cancer was left at the cut margins, which is the good part. The scan of your
> abdomen didn't show any spread to the liver. The one thing that stands out is that of the 14 lymph
> nodes they checked, 2 had cancer cells in them. That's not a disaster, but it's the detail your
> team will want to talk through, because node involvement is what they weigh when deciding whether
> to offer treatment after surgery — which is almost certainly what the 2-week appointment is for.
> Your haemoglobin is also a little low (11.8) — worth a mention so they keep an eye on it, but
> nothing for you to act on.
>
> A couple of things the letter just doesn't tell us, and that I'd gently make sure of:
> - whether they've run the MMR/MSI test on the tumour — it's recommended for everyone newly
>   diagnosed with bowel cancer, and it can shape treatment;
> - whether the case went to the multidisciplinary team (the MDT, where the specialists discuss it
>   together);
> - whether staging is complete — usually that's a CT of the chest as well as the abdomen, plus a
>   baseline CEA blood test.
>
> None of that means anything's wrong — it's just making sure nothing was skipped before the
> treatment conversation. (This is based on current bowel-cancer guidelines as of mid-2026 — your
> team will have the very latest.) Easy, non-confrontational ways to ask at the appointment:
> - "Has the tumour been tested for MMR/MSI, and what did it show?"
> - "Was our case discussed at an MDT, and what did they suggest?"
> - "Is the staging complete — was there a chest CT and a CEA — and what stage are we looking at?"
> - "Given the involved nodes, what are the after-surgery options, and the pros and cons for us?"
>
> Want me to put those into a short list you can take with you, and start a running note so you don't
> have to hold all this in your head? And just so it's clear — I'm here to help you understand and
> prepare, not to diagnose or decide treatment; your team does that with you.

### Caregiver, 3 a.m. — conversational voice (new mode demo)

> *Input:* "It's 3 a.m. and I can't sleep. I keep reading survival stats and I feel like I'm not
> doing enough. Her scan is in two weeks and I'm terrified."

> Hey — first, that exhaustion is real, and being awake at 3 a.m. reading survival stats doesn't
> mean you're failing her. It means you love her and your brain won't switch off. Let's slow it down.
>
> The honest truth about that reading: those survival numbers are about big groups of people, not
> about her — they can't tell you her story, and at 3 a.m. they really only do one thing, which is
> make the fear louder. If the last hour of searching hasn't changed a decision or given you a
> concrete next step, that's worry talking, not new information — and it's genuinely okay to stop for
> tonight.
>
> Nothing here needs solving before morning. The scan in two weeks is a real date, but not a tonight
> date. If it helps, let me hold the things spinning in your head — tell me what they are and I'll
> keep them in a list, so you can put them down and pick them up tomorrow with a clearer head.
>
> One small thing right now if you want it: a slow breath, hand on your chest, in for four, out for
> six, a few times. Then sleep — you'll handle the next two weeks far better rested than wired.
>
> And if the sleeplessness and dread keep going, that isn't weakness, it's load — caregivers carry an
> enormous amount, and there are people whose job is to help *you* specifically (a psycho-oncologist,
> or a caregiver support line). I can point you to some when you're ready. For tonight: you're doing
> enough. Go rest.
>
> (If she ever has something urgent — a high fever during treatment, heavy bleeding, trouble
> breathing, sudden confusion — that's the one thing not to sit with: call the care team or
> emergency line straight away. Nothing like that here tonight.)

### Chordoma — conversational voice (v2)

> Okay, let's walk through this letter together — it's a lot, but the heart of it is manageable.
>
> In plain terms: after the operation on the chordoma (a rare bone tumour at the base of the skull),
> the scan shows some tumour is still there (residual), and the centre wants to do
> radiotherapy next — standard photon radiotherapy (IMRT) at their unit, about 70 Gy over roughly 35
> sessions, starting in around four weeks. For chordoma, this radiotherapy step really matters — it's
> a big part of keeping the tumour in check.
>
> One thing I'd gently flag, since you asked about next steps. When I look at how chordoma is usually
> treated, there's a more specialised type of radiotherapy — particle therapy (protons or carbon
> ions) — that guidelines often point to for cases like this, because it can give a higher dose to
> the tumour while better sparing the brainstem right next to it. Your letter doesn't mention whether
> it was considered. That doesn't mean the photon plan is wrong — there may be a perfectly good reason
> for it — but it could be worth asking, and if you'd like, I can help you find centres that offer it
> so you could get a second opinion. (This is based on current guidelines as of mid-2026 —
> guideline-recommended rather than proven by a single trial, so worth confirming the latest with
> your team.)
>
> And if particle therapy does turn out to be the better fit but isn't available where you are, you're
> not stuck: in the EU, planned treatment abroad can sometimes be funded — through the S2 route (your
> insurer pays the foreign public centre directly) or reimbursement under the cross-border directive.
> That's something to ask your team and your country's national contact point about — not something to
> arrange alone on a hunch.
>
> Because the start is only about four weeks away, timing matters, so I'd raise it before
> radiotherapy begins. Some calm ways to put it:
> - "We've read particle therapy is often recommended for chordoma — is it right for our case, or is
>   there a clinical reason photon therapy is better here?"
> - "Was our case discussed at a sarcoma multidisciplinary team?"
> - "If particle therapy fits but isn't available here, is referral to a specialised centre —
>   including abroad — an option, and would it fit the timing?"
>
> Want me to note this as an open question and a reminder to ask before the radiotherapy date — and,
> if it helps, start a short running note so you're not holding all of this in your head? And to be
> clear: I'm helping you understand and prepare, not deciding anything — your team does that with you.
